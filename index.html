What 10 million transactions say about which alerts get escalated

01 · Approach

Treat every alert as one customer's 180-day story

The transaction table is relational: each alert (signal_id) owns hundreds of historical transactions, and the label lives at the alert level. So the whole problem is a question of summarising a history into evidence. We worked in five passes.

Structure first. Before comparing classes we established what a history actually is: a fixed 180-day lookback ending at the alert date, a compressed burst of activity in the last minutes before the alert, and no customer appearing behind more than one alert. Those facts decide which features can exist at all.

Compare everything by class. Timing, direction, transaction type, size distributions, sequence motifs and calendar effects were each tested for a difference between escalated and dismissed alerts, using per-alert aggregates and one-feature ROC-AUC as the common yardstick.

Follow the size index. Almost all of the signal turned out to live in transaction sizes: how large a customer's bank transfers are relative to their cash and card activity, which kinds of transactions dominate the largest amounts, and two narrow bands of near-zero amounts that behave like hidden customer flags.

Build, validate, blend. About 1,200 candidate features across six families fed gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM, CatBoost, XGBoost) and a regularised logistic model, blended into one probability. Feature selection was checked with nested cross-validation so the reported score is honest.

Ask why the flags work. In a second round we treated the two near-zero size bands not as features but as clues. They mark two latent customer segments with distinct everyday behaviour, so a label-free model can recognise segment members whose flag rows never appeared. The rest of that round — a dozen further ideas, each scored on the same folds — mapped where the ceiling of this data is (sections 06–08).

Result in one line. The blended model reaches a cross-validated ROC-AUC of 0.667 (round 1: 0.663; 0.657 under a fully nested protocol). Alerts in the top predicted decile escalate at 32%, against 8% in the bottom decile — a meaningful reordering of reviewer effort on a target that carries a lot of irreducible noise. The second round added +0.003 AUC (90% bootstrap interval +0.001 to +0.005) and, more importantly, an explanation of why the score stops there.

Three structural facts shaped everything that follows.

Every history is a 180-day window ending at the alert date. The earliest transaction of an alert sits at most 180.0 days before its date, and the whole transaction table starts exactly 180 days before the first alert date (2024-07-05 vs 2025-01-01). History length is therefore not a feature; the window is identical for everyone.

Train and test are a random split of the same population. Alerts per month follow the same curve at a 70/30 ratio, transaction counts per alert have the same shape, and every summary statistic we later relied on matches to two decimals between the two sets (table below). Nothing suggests temporal drift, so a plain stratified split is a faithful proxy for the leaderboard.

No customer appears behind two alerts. Across all 10 million rows, not a single (timestamp, direction, type, size) row is shared between two signal_ids. Histories cannot be linked, and labels cannot leak between alerts — each alert has to be judged on its own history.

03 · Activity over time

A slow ramp, then three frantic minutes

Relative to the alert date, activity is far from uniform. Weekly transaction share rises the further back one looks, peaks around 18 weeks before the alert, and thins out in the final month — the same hump for escalated and dismissed alerts. The center of mass of a history has a one-feature AUC of 0.500: the shape is a property of the data generator, not of risk.

Week 0 is different. 7.9% of all transactions happen in the last 180 seconds before midnight of the alert date (from 23:57:00 to 23:59:59), spread perfectly uniformly across those seconds. This "burst" is present for every alert, in both classes, and its fine timing carries no information (AUC 0.49–0.51 for any burst-timing statistic). We read it as the alert-triggering activity being compressed onto the alert day during data transformation, and treated it as a separate segment ("burst") rather than as ordinary history.

Hour of day and weekday are flat (every hour holds 4.17% ± 0.01% of history transactions), so night-time or weekend behaviour is not part of this dataset's story.

04 · Incoming vs outgoing, and by type

Counted by rows the customers receive; weighed by size they transfer

Three quarters of rows are incoming (kirim) and card transactions (karta) are the most frequent type. Weighted by size the picture inverts: bank transfers (bank_otkazmasi) are 40% of rows but 72% of volume, and international transfers (xalqaro) are 0.45% of rows yet a fifth of volume. Cash (naqd) is small either way, at 6% of rows and 3% of volume.

Volume here uses exp(2 × miqdor_indeksi) as an amount proxy. The size index behaves like a standardised logarithm of the amount (section 05), and among the scalings we tried, this one produced the most informative volume shares — it is the scale on which "which transactions dominate the money" becomes visible.

The burst is card-heavier than history (60% vs 53% of rows) and consists of smaller transactions; the direction mix is unchanged. By class, the raw mixes barely move: escalated alerts have 0.4 points more outgoing rows and 0.2 points more cash rows. Mix on its own is not the signal.

05 · Transaction sizes

The size index is where the classes differ

miqdor_indeksi is a standardised size indicator (mean −0.13, standard deviation 0.98, range −2.91 to 6.69). Its shape per type is close to normal on this scale, which is what a log-amount looks like: international transfers sit around +2, cash around +0.5, incoming card payments around −0.5. Two artefacts anchor the scale: a hard floor at −2.9123 reached only by incoming bank transfers (a zero amount), and per-type caps (6.69 for bank transfers, 4.18 for cards).

Within a customer, the standardised shape of each type's sizes is identical across classes — the same bell curve to three decimals. What differs is the level: escalated alerts belong to customers whose bank transfers are smaller (−0.13 index units for both directions) and whose international transfers are smaller (−0.2), while their cash and card amounts sit at the same level as everyone else's. In amount terms, escalated customers move less money through the bank while handling ordinary amounts of cash and card spend.

06 · Target distribution & behavioural patterns

A stable 17% base rate, and five patterns that move it

2,405 of 14,000 training alerts (17.2%) were escalated. The rate is flat over time — monthly rates range 14–21% and a χ² test against month gives p = 0.09; day of week, day of month and the numeric order of signal_id show nothing. There is no leakage to exploit in the alert table itself; the label must be predicted from behaviour.

Behaviour, in turn, offers no single strong tell. The best individual round-1 feature reaches an AUC of 0.61 (0.64 for the round-2 segment score). The signal is diffuse, spread over five related patterns that all concern transaction sizes.

Pattern 1 — bank transfers small relative to cash and card

The strongest single predictor is a difference of levels: the customer's average outgoing bank-transfer size minus their average cash-deposit size (AUC 0.613; lower → escalate). Bank minus card behaves the same way (0.609). A customer whose banking is small next to their cash and card activity is the profile reviewers escalate.

The same idea expressed as money shares — the fraction of volume that is cash deposits (0.599) or card spending (0.595) — is nearly as strong, and combining count with level is what the tree models spend most of their splits on.

Pattern 2 — the identity of the largest transactions

Because amounts are heavy-tailed, a handful of transactions carry most of the money, and what kind they are matters. When an alert's single largest transaction is an outgoing bank transfer, 13.9% escalate; when it is a card payment, 24.1%; a cash deposit, 23.3%. Counting cash deposits among the ten largest transactions moves the rate from 13.9% (none) to 29% (six or more) — a clean monotonic gradient.

Pattern 3 — micro-amount transactions are a risk flag

Scanning the size index at fine resolution revealed narrow bands just above each type's minimum where escalation is roughly double the base rate: incoming bank transfers between −2.897 and −2.875, outgoing bank transfers between −2.81 and −2.77, and the smallest card payments in either direction. Rows in these bands belong to escalated alerts 27–37% of the time. The bands co-occur across types (correlation 0.3–0.4 between their presence flags), which reads as one latent customer property — a history sprinkled with near-zero test-like transactions — rather than four separate quirks.

Alerts with no micro-band transactions escalate at 14.8%; with one, 25%; with six to eight, 36%.

Pattern 4 — the zero-amount floor is protective, and it wins

Immediately below the micro band sits the floor cluster: incoming bank transfers at the minimum of the scale (−2.912 to −2.897, i.e. a zero or near-zero amount) and their outgoing counterpart. These are not the tail of the normal size distribution — customers show far fewer of them than a clipped bell curve would predict — and they point the other way: 18% of alerts have at least one, and only 10% of those escalate against 18.5% for the rest. Controlling for size level does not remove the effect, and when an alert carries both floor and micro-band transactions the floor dominates (9.4% escalation).

The chart on the right shows the boundary at the row level: the density of incoming bank-transfer sizes near the floor, and the escalation rate of the alerts those rows belong to. The rate steps from ≈10% to ≈30% at −2.897 within a span of 0.005 index units.

Pattern 5 — the flags mark customer segments, and the segments have hidden members

The two bands co-occur in a telling way. Floor rows of incoming and outgoing bank transfers appear together (64% of alerts with an outgoing floor row also have an incoming one), micro-band rows of the four bank and card categories appear together (29–58%), and the two families never mix: not one alert with a floor row has a micro-band card row. Read as a partition, the training alerts fall into three segments — F (any floor row, 18.0%, escalation 10.0%), R (micro-band rows only, 17.1%, escalation 29.3%) and N (neither, 65%, escalation 16.0%) — with the same shares in the test set.

Inside F the label is unpredictable: our final model scores AUC 0.51 there, i.e. floor customers are escalated at a flat 10% whatever else their history contains. Inside R it reaches 0.54 (the number of micro-band rows still matters), and inside N it reaches 0.66 — that is where the size-level patterns of section 05 live: across deciles of the bank-out minus cash-in level, the escalation rate of N alerts falls from 26% to 10%, while in R the same feature barely moves it.

Marker rows are rare — a segment member emits about one per hundred bank rows — so whether an alert shows its marker depends on how long its history is. The share of alerts with a floor row rises from 4.5% among customers with at most 25 incoming bank transfers to 24.7% among those with 300–500, and the micro-band share from 3.9% to 22.6%. A segment member with a short history usually looks like N; by this curve roughly a third of the true F and R members are hidden that way.

Segments also differ in everyday behaviour: F and R customers have lower bank-transfer, card and cash levels than N customers (mean bank level -0.05 and -0.16 against +0.26), and F customers keep larger outgoing transfers than R customers. So we trained an auxiliary segment model: a multiclass LightGBM that predicts the observed marker segment from bulk behaviour alone — levels, spreads and mixes computed on the non-marker rows — out-of-fold over all 20,000 alerts, without ever seeing the escalation label. It separates F and R from the rest with ROC-AUC 0.91 each. Applied to the marker-free alerts it exposes the hidden members: sorted by its F↔R log-odds, their escalation rate climbs from 8.8% in the most F-like decile to 28.9% in the most R-like. That log-odds is the strongest single feature we found (AUC 0.644 on its own, against 0.613 for the best round-1 feature) and the one addition of the second round that improved the model.

What we looked for and did not find. Recency (activity in the last 7/30/90 days versus older), velocity spikes, inter-arrival gaps, night and weekend shares, pass-through pairs (money in then out within hours), category-transition motifs, repeated identical amounts and calendar effects all scored within 0.47–0.53 AUC individually and added nothing in the model. The escalation decision in this data is about who the customer is — a stable size profile — not about what happened last week.

Two round-1 ideas that looked promising were also rejected by validation. A transaction-level model (score every row, then average per alert) collapsed to AUC 0.52 because it memorised per-alert context instead of learning row-level evidence; and a Gaussian-mixture attempt to recover discrete customer segments found only "which types the customer uses", scoring 0.57. The per-alert aggregate representation is the right one for this data.

08 · Modelling & validation

Shallow boosted trees, blended, validated twice

All 1,282 features go to gradient-boosted tree models kept deliberately shallow (4–7 leaves, 100–200 minimum samples per leaf, 20–30% feature subsampling, L2 regularisation), because the signal is weak and diffuse; a dozen hyper-parameter settings all landed within 0.003 AUC of each other. Three LightGBM variants (4–6 seeds each), CatBoost (depth 4, 3 seeds), XGBoost (2 seeds) and a regularised logistic regression on the 150 strongest features are averaged with equal weights into the final probability. Each model's test prediction is the mean of its five fold models, so the submitted probabilities come from exactly the models that were validated.

Validation uses stratified 5-fold cross-validation with the same folds for every model. Because the strongest feature subset was chosen with the help of importance scores, we also ran a nested protocol (selection repeated inside each outer fold): it returned 0.657 against 0.666 for the optimistic version, so the numbers we report are the honest ones. In round 2 we repeated that nested test for top-40 to top-300 subsets: 0.661–0.662, against 0.668 when the subset is chosen on the full training set — so a feature subset serves only as one blend member, never as the model. Fold-to-fold variation is ±0.015, which is also the uncertainty one should attach to a 6,000-alert leaderboard score; the round-2 blend beats the round-1 blend by +0.003 on the same folds (paired bootstrap over alerts, 90% interval +0.001 to +0.005).

09 · Conclusion

What we learned

Structure

Every alert is one customer's fixed 180-day history plus a compressed three-minute burst on the alert day. Train and test are the same population, and no customer spans two alerts — so per-alert summaries, validated by a plain stratified split, are the honest way to model this.

Time

Timing carries no risk information: the activity ramp, the burst, hours and weekdays are identical across classes. The escalation decision is about a stable customer profile, not about recent change.

Sizes

Almost all signal is in the size index. Escalated customers move smaller bank and international transfers while their cash and card amounts are ordinary; the most predictive single number is the gap between bank-transfer level and cash-deposit level.

Money

Because amounts are heavy-tailed, the identity of the few largest transactions matters: cash deposits or card spending at the top of the list roughly double the escalation rate relative to bank transfers at the top.

Flags

Two narrow bands at the bottom of the size scale act as hidden customer flags — micro-amount transactions (risk up to 36%) and zero-amount incoming transfers (risk down to 10%, and dominant when both are present). They are equally common in the test set.

Segments

The two flags are the visible tip of three customer segments. Floor customers (18%) are escalated at a flat 10% whatever they do, micro-band customers (17%) at 29%, and the remaining two thirds carry all of the size-level signal. Membership shows in everyday behaviour, so a label-free auxiliary model recovers members whose flag rows never appeared — the one idea of the second round that moved the score.

Model

A blend of shallow boosted trees and a logistic model reaches 0.667 cross-validated ROC-AUC (round 1: 0.663; 0.657 nested), ranking the riskiest decile at 32% escalation versus 8% for the safest. The remaining gap to a perfect score is label noise the transaction history does not explain: within the floor segment the label is a coin with a 10% bias, and a learning curve over the training set has flattened.

Limitations and next steps

The data is synthetic and its artefacts (the burst, the floor, the bands) are properties of the generator rather than of Uzbek payment behaviour; a production model would have to re-derive such thresholds. With a 17% base rate and 14,000 alerts, differences of ±0.01 AUC are within fold noise, so we chose regularisation and blending over aggressive tuning. Round 2 tested the likelihood-based (empirical-Bayes) estimate of each customer's per-type size level that round 1 had left open; it did not help, because with hundreds of rows per alert the sample means are already precise and the remaining error is in the labels, not in the features. What remains open is calibrating the probabilities for reviewer workload planning rather than only for ranking, and — outside this competition — checking whether real alert data carries the segment structure that this generator built in.
